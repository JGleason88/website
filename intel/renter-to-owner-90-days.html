First-time buyers

Renter to owner in 90 days.

Most renters think they're years away from a down payment. Many are about 90 days away from being buyer-ready. Here's the plan, with real numbers.

By Josh GleasonNovember 12, 20266 min read

The problem isn't your income. It's the plan.

If you earn a steady income and pay rent on time, you may be closer to owning than you think. You don't need 20% down. On a $575,000 home, the minimum down payment is $32,500: 5% of the first $500,000 and 10% of the rest.

What most renters are missing isn't income. It's a plan that uses three government tools together, plus the tax refund those tools create.

Tool 1: the FHSA

The First Home Savings Account lets you put in up to $8,000 a year, to a $40,000 lifetime limit. Contributions are tax-deductible, and withdrawals for your first home are tax-free. You never pay it back.

Open it before December 31. Contribution room only starts the year you open the account. Open it this year and you can contribute for 2026 now, and again in January.

Tool 2: the Home Buyers' Plan

The Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) lets first-time buyers take up to $60,000 out of their RRSP tax-free for a down payment, or $120,000 for a couple. You repay it to your RRSP over 15 years, and for withdrawals made by the end of 2028, repayments don't start until the fifth year.

There's one rule that matters: money has to sit in your RRSP for at least 90 days before you can take it out under the HBP. That's where the 90 days comes from.

Tool 3: the RRSP loan

Most people with RRSP contribution room don't have the cash to fill it. An RRSP loan fixes that. The bank lends you money, up to your unused RRSP room, and it goes straight into your RRSP. That contribution gets you a tax deduction, and 90 days later you can withdraw it under the HBP for your down payment.

Your RRSP room is on your CRA My Account or your last Notice of Assessment. It builds up at 18% of your earned income each year, so if you've worked for a few years and never contributed, you probably have plenty.

The tax refund on top

Everything you put into your FHSA and RRSP is deducted from your taxable income. Your employer already took tax off your pay as if you'd never made those contributions, so when you file, much of that tax comes back as a refund. That refund goes on top of your down payment.

A worked example: a Barrie couple

Two renters in Barrie, each earning $70,000 a year, with $24,000 saved between them and at least $30,000 of unused RRSP room each.

Before December 31

  • Each opens an FHSA and puts in $8,000: $16,000 of their savings.
  • Each takes a $30,000 RRSP loan and contributes it: $60,000 into their RRSPs.
  • The last $8,000 of savings stays in the bank for closing costs.

February to March

  • They file their 2026 taxes early. Each of them deducted $38,000, so each gets back about $8,950.
  • Ninety days after the RRSP contributions, the money is eligible for the Home Buyers' Plan.

What about the RRSP loans?

The loans are still there after closing, and they're the trade-off in this plan. Two options:

  • Put the refund into the down payment, as in this example, and pay the loans off over a few years.
  • Use the refund to pay the loans down first. Your down payment is smaller, but a lower monthly loan payment can help you qualify for more.

The right choice depends on your numbers, which is exactly what the assessment is for.

Your 90-day plan

  1. Day 1: free assessment. What you qualify for today, your RRSP room, and your down payment target.
