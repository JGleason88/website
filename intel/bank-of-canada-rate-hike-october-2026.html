Rates just went up. Here's what it does to your payment.

The Bank of Canada raised its rate for the first time in more than three years. Whether you feel it this month depends on what kind of mortgage you have.

What happened

On October 28, the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate by a quarter point, from 2.25% to 2.50%. It's the Bank's first increase since July 2023. Within hours, Canada's big banks raised their prime rates from 4.45% to 4.70%, effective October 29.

The reason: inflation. The Bank said price growth has been running hotter and stickier than it expected, sitting around 3% since spring, against its 2% target. Raising rates is how it tries to cool that down.

Who feels it, and how much

1. Variable rate, adjustable payment

If your payment moves when prime moves, it's going up now. Here's the math on a variable rate going from 3.70% to 3.95% (prime minus 0.75%), with a 25-year amortization:

Mortgage balanceBeforeAfterChange / month
$300,000$1,534$1,575+$41
$500,000$2,557$2,625+$68
$700,000$3,580$3,676+$96

Estimates, rounded. Rule of thumb: about $14 more per month for every $100,000 you owe. Your lender's notice shows your exact new payment.

2. Variable rate, fixed payment

Your payment doesn't change, but more of it now goes to interest and less to paying down your balance. That stretches out how long it takes to pay off. If rates keep rising, you can hit your lender's trigger rate, the point where your payment no longer covers the interest. Ask your lender where yours sits.

3. Lines of credit and HELOCs

Most are tied directly to prime, so the increase lands right away. On interest-only payments, that's about $21 more per month for every $100,000 borrowed.

4. Fixed rate

Nothing changes until your renewal. Fixed rates don't follow the Bank of Canada directly; they mostly track the bond market, so they can move before or after a Bank of Canada decision.

5. Renewing in the next year

This is where the decision really matters. Most lenders will hold a rate for you for 90 to 120 days before renewal, which protects you if rates keep climbing. The renewal letter your bank mails you is a starting offer, not the best one available.

Bottom line: one quarter-point hike is a small bump on its own. What matters is what comes next. The Bank's next decision is December 9, and it left the door open to more increases without promising one.

My take

If you're on a variable rate and the extra $50 to $100 a month stings, look at locking in before the next decision instead of waiting to see what happens. If you're renewing in the next 12 months, get a rate hold now. It costs nothing and only works in your favour. And if you're carrying a big HELOC balance, this is a good time to look at rolling it into your mortgage at a lower rate.
